Abstract. We coded 137 LLM inference-optimization papers (2022–26, purposive sample), read in full, for what each design optimizes, constrains or only reports. Throughput is the commonest objective (57 of 137, 42 %); token pace and quality are mostly constraints. What a paper optimizes follows its technique layer and load regime: 60 of 137 never evaluate under online arrivals. Tails enter design mainly as distributional SLOs (32 of 77 online- or mixed-load papers), and only 2 papers minimize a latency percentile. Metric names do not fix quantities: TTFT, goodput and token throughput each have rival definitions, and one metric paper shows the metric reversing a capacity ranking. Routers that minimize tail latency exist (an online-linear-programming router, OLP, and GORGO), and one shows a tail objective being gamed by concentrating load.

01 · AnswerThe short answer

Papers maximize throughput most often; tails enter as distributional SLOs, rarely as the objective.

Three numbers carry the answer. Throughput (tokens or requests per second) is an objective in 57 of 137 full-text optimizer papers, 42 % [33.7–50.0] [D] obj_con_by_family.json · throughput. Among the 77 papers evaluated under online or mixed load, 32 (42 % [31.2–52.7]) set a distributional latency target: a percentile SLO, an attainment level, goodput, or a per-token deadline [D] tail_tiers.json · online_mixed|2a. Only 2 of the 137 make a latency percentile the quantity they minimize: GORGO and Autellix [D] tail_tiers.json · all|1a. Three more (Preble, Kairos, PolyServe) name tails as a design aim without a percentile objective [D] all|1b.

The two "42 %" figures are different quantities over different populations. The first is a share of all 137 papers; the second is a share of the 77 online- or mixed-load papers. They coincide by chance.

The six findings

1. Throughput is the default objective; token pace and quality are mostly constraints. Token pace (TPOT or TBT) is a constraint in 26 papers (19 %) and an objective in 8 (6 %); quality is a constraint in 36 (26 %) and an objective in 3 (2 %) [D] obj_con_by_family.json. Other latencies are split. TTFT is an objective in 19 and a constraint in 20; request latency is an objective in 19 and a constraint in 10. Only 20 of the 57 throughput-objective papers also state a latency constraint [D] paper_table.csv; ORCH_NOTES, so "throughput under latency constraints" describes schedulers and P/D systems well and the corpus as a whole poorly.

2. Technique layer and load regime set what a paper optimizes. Request schedulers make throughput an objective in 14 of 23; speculative decoders make single-sequence speed an objective in 9 of 13 [D] layer_family_heatmap.json. The load regime is the sharper divide: 60 of 137 papers (44 %) never evaluate under online arrivals [D] load_class.json · ALL|_never_online, which covers all 14 quantization and KV-compression papers. Their speed-ups exclude queueing.

3. The tail is rarely the quantity minimized. It enters as a distributional target over requests or tokens. Some of those targets constrain how per-request means are spread: about 6–8 papers set SLOs on per-request mean TPOT (two of them mixed), while about 13 constrain token-level timing (percentiles of gaps, a per-iteration budget, a per-token bound or per-token deadlines) [Syn/D] ORCH_NOTES, Erratum v6.1b; SYNTHESIS v6.1. Routers that target tails exist: GORGO, and the online-linear-programming (OLP) router of Chen et al.; the one RL router in the corpus (Intelligent Router) does not.

4. Metric names do not pin down quantities. Over the 143 full-text records available when the taxonomy was built, TTFT is timed from arrival in 16 papers, defined as prefill duration in 15, and left without a start point in 19–20; goodput has at least 8 definitions; token throughput counts tokens four ways plus "unstated" [D] def_variants.json. 40 of 143 (28 %) use a headline metric they never define [D] DEFINITIONS.md §3.

5. Headlines often name a narrower quantity than their words imply. EAGLE-3's "up to 6.5×" is a single-sequence speedup over vanilla autoregressive decoding; its fixed-batch throughput gain in SGLang (no request arrivals) is 1.81× at batch 2 and 1.38× at batch 64 [P] arXiv 2503.01840 Tables 1, 3. SmoothQuant's "end-to-end latency" is one prefill pass over a batch of 4 [P] arXiv 2211.10438 §5.3.

6. The expected metric list needs four additions [Syn]: single-sequence decode speed (not a serving latency), program or workflow completion time, token-timeline QoE metrics that penalize stalls, and mechanism proxies optimized directly, such as cache hit rate and model start-up time. Section 4 gives the full list.

For tail-aware routing

Tail objectives in routing are rare but not new: the OLP router folds tail thresholds into per-request rewards, and GORGO minimizes p95 TTFT with a prefix-aware cost model (§11, Table 7). Work in this area is read against goodput at 90 % joint attainment and MLPerf-style P99 gates, so it should report goodput beside its P99s [Syn] SYNTHESIS v6.1.

What the slide simplifies

"Most often" means the largest single family. It is short of a majority: 42 % is below half, and its interval tops out at 50 %. "Tails enter as distributional SLOs" describes how tails enter when they enter at all. 64 of 137 papers (47 % [38.6–55.0]) set no serving-latency target of any kind [D] tail_tiers.json · all|5, mostly because they are evaluated without arrivals. The tier-1a count is a strict reading set by hand; the R5 reviewer noted that Autellix's P95/P99 is the measure of an anti-starvation goal, so a stricter reader could make it 1 [Syn] notes/R5_review.md.

02 · MethodHow the numbers were made

We coded 137 optimizer papers from full text and checked the coding blind.

Corpus

Six discovery agents, one per layer cluster (scheduling; cluster level; KV cache; decoding and compression; kernels, runtime and hardware; agentic serving plus metric papers), produced 216 rows and 210 unique papers [D] candidates/D1–D6.tsv.

Selection was purposive: canonical, high-uptake or venue-published work, with quotas of at least 60 % 2024–26 and at least 30 % 2025–26 papers in each cluster. 133 optimizers were read in full, then 4 routing and scheduling papers were added after review R1 (GORGO, the OLP router, PPD, ExeGPT) for 137 [D] SYNTHESIS v6.1 §1. Ten metric or benchmark papers were read in full for definitions only. Sixty papers went to an abstract track, of which 55 had a usable abstract; 7 non-optimizers were excluded.

By first arXiv version, the 137 split 2022: 8, 2023: 26, 2024: 57, 2025: 40, 2026: 6; the trend buckets 2022–23 / 2024 / 2025–26 hold 34 / 57 / 46 [D] ORCH_NOTES. Request scheduling is the largest layer (23), then agent and compound serving (16), cluster routing, parallelism/offload/hardware and speculative decoding (13 each); the full per-layer counts appear as row labels in Figure 5. One skew matters for trends: no 2025–26 quantization or KV-compression paper was read in full; those went to the abstract track.

Coding rules

Each record lists every metric a paper optimizes with a role. An objective is what the method is designed to improve and must be backed by a verbatim design statement (problem formulation, objective function, reward, policy goal or stated design goal). A constraint is what the method must respect, such as an SLO, a quality floor or a memory budget. Reported metrics are evaluated or claimed in a headline but not targeted. After review R1 every record was re-passed: objectives backed only by a headline result were demoted to reported (about 48 entries), statistics were set to "unspecified" unless the paper states them, and a load class was recorded per paper [D] RESEARCH_LOG.md.

Seven normalization rules (N1–N7) are applied in the aggregation script without editing records. Examples: prefill latency and queued TTFT join the first-token family (N1); Melange's "TPOT", defined as latency divided by output tokens, is recoded as normalized latency (N2); year is the first arXiv version (N7). Analysis is at the level of 16 metric families (15 named families plus mechanism/other). Different groupings move individual shares but not the ordering, and merging goodput into throughput would widen throughput's lead [Syn] SYNTHESIS v6.1 §1.

Reliability

A second agent re-extracted 30 records blind. The primary objective matched in 28 of 30, the mean Jaccard overlap of objective codes was 0.74, and the objective sets and the constraint sets each matched exactly in 14 of 30 [D] qa/QA_COMPARISON_v2.txt. A blind coder assigned tail tiers to 30 papers (23 online/mixed, 7 other) from the full texts alone and agreed on 25 (Cohen's κ = 0.76); on "tier 1 or 2a vs the rest" it agreed on 29 [D] qa/TIER_QA_COMPARISON.txt. A tail-role audit read 40 papers with recorded or likely tail entries, and 181 definition quotes were checked against the full texts (161 automatically, 20 by hand) [D] qa/TAIL_AUDIT.md; DEFINITIONS.md. Appendix A gives the procedures.

The reading is: primary objectives are reliable; exact objective and constraint code sets are noisier, so the report leans on family-level shares. The R2 reviewer judged about 7 of 45 checked design quotes (≈ 15 %) to be motivation or evaluation sentences rather than design statements [Syn] notes/R2_review.md.

Why full texts were needed

Discovery agents coded each candidate's abstract before any full-text read. On the same 133 papers, the abstract-level objective code set matched the full-text set exactly in 16 (mean Jaccard 0.43). At least one objective family was shared in 124 of 133, but the family sets were identical in only 30 [D] ORCH_NOTES; summary_v3.txt. Abstracts usually name a right family and rarely the variant or the statistic. On the abstract track itself, about 20 of 55 records say only "latency" or "speedup" abstract only. The screening field asked only what is optimized, so it cannot show whether abstracts reveal constraints.

What the slide simplifies

The slide's "checked blind" measures coding agreement; two coders can agree on a wrong code. The 25/30 tier agreement covers only one tier-1 paper (GORGO); tier-1 membership was set by hand from the tail audit and reviews R2–R3. The reliability figures were measured on the records as they stood after the re-pass; later hand corrections (for example Preble, Kairos) are not re-sampled.

03 · DefinitionsThe metrics on one timeline

On one request's timeline, TPOT averages the gaps that TBT counts one by one.

Notation [Syn] DEFINITIONS.md §0. A request arrives at time a and emits output tokens at t₁ … tₙ. Then TTFT = t₁ − a; the gaps are gᵢ = tᵢ − tᵢ₋₁ for i = 2 … n; TPOT = (tₙ − t₁)/(n − 1), the mean gap; and E2E = tₙ − a. Two identities follow directly:

  • E2E = TTFT + (n − 1)·TPOT, exactly, per request. DistServe's footnote states the same identity: "The overall request latency equals TTFT plus TPOT times the number of generated tokens in the decoding phase", and the decoding phase generates n − 1 tokens [P] arXiv 2401.09670 fn 1.
  • Normalized latency = E2E / n = TTFT/n + TPOT·(n − 1)/n. It is a length-weighted mix of first-token and decode latency, so it moves when the output-length distribution moves [Syn].

Where TTFT starts

The consensus definition starts TTFT at arrival, so under load it contains queueing, scheduling, any KV-cache fetch, and prefill. Etalon states it plainly: "Time To First Token (TTFT) [32, 7] is the latency between the request arrival and the first output token generated by the system for the request. It includes the scheduling delay (time elapsed from request arrival to start of prompt processing) and the prompt processing time." [P] arXiv 2407.07000 §2.2

Fifteen papers instead define TTFT as the prefill duration. DistServe writes "the time to first token (TTFT), which is the duration of the prefill phase" [P] arXiv 2401.09670 §1, yet its latency model and measurements include queueing; it is one of 5 prefill-defined papers that measure under load anyway [D] def_variants.json · TTFT|T2a. A prefill kernel and a scheduler can both report a "TTFT gain"; only the arrival-based one contains the queueing a router controls.

Two further pitfalls. Preble drops TTFT and TPOT as key metrics because "our target LLM use has short output lengths, rendering TPOT not as meaningful and TTFT close to the request latency." [P] arXiv 2407.00023 §4.2 (Metrics) And because TTFT grows with prompt length, 8 papers scale the TTFT SLO with prompt length; Etalon warns that dividing TTFT by prompt length "normalizes the scheduling delay as well and would penalize shorter input requests disproportionately" [P] arXiv 2407.07000 §3.1.

TPOT, TBT and the per-request P99 of a request's own gaps

These are three different random variables. TPOT has one value per request; DistServe defines it as the time per output token, "which represents the average time taken to generate a token for each request (except for the first token)" [P] arXiv 2401.09670 §1. Its P99 is a tail over requests.

TBT (each gap; vLLM calls it ITL) treats every gap as a sample; Sarathi-Serve: "TBT on the other hand measures the interval between the generation of consecutive output tokens of a request, and affects the overall perceived fluidity of the response." [P] arXiv 2403.02310 §2.4 Its P99 is a tail over tokens. ConServe states the relation: "Note that TBT is measured per token and is stricter than time per output token (TPOT), which averages the TBT across all tokens per request." [P] arXiv 2410.01228 §2 fn 1

A third form takes a percentile of each request's own gaps and then passes or fails the request. Apt-Serve: "P99 TBT refers to the 99th percentile of TBT latency for each individual request" [P] arXiv 2504.07494 §3.1. Its attainment counts requests whose own gap tail stays under the bound, which is neither a token-level P99 nor a request-level P99 of means. Five papers call an average "TBT" or "ITL", so the label alone does not tell you which was computed [D] DEFINITIONS.md §1.2–1.3.

The harnesses

MLPerf Inference times latency "from when the LoadGen was scheduled to pass a query to the SUT, to the time it receives a reply" [P] MLPerf rules, so its TTFT starts at the scheduled arrival and includes all queueing in the system under test; it computes TPOT per sample as (latency − TTFT)/(n − 1).

vLLM's bench serve, SGLang's benchmark and GenAI-Perf start TTFT at request send, so they include server-side queueing but not any wait before the client sends; vLLM reports that client-side wait separately. NVIDIA's guide says TTFT "generally includes request queuing time, prefill time, and network latency" [P] notes/INDUSTRY_DEFS.md §1–4. The harnesses therefore agree that TTFT includes server-side queueing, and they agree on TPOT; they disagree on ITL, throughput accounting and goodput (§13, Table 9).

What the slide simplifies

The slide draws a client-side view in which each gap is visible. Several papers use the per-step execution time of the decode iteration as "TBT" or "TPOT" (ConServe, MegaScale-Infer, Step-3, Medha; CloudMatrix384 reports a fixed-batch decode TPOT); that equals the observed gap only when the request is in every iteration, and misses waits caused by preemption [P] DEFINITIONS.md §1.2–1.3, P4. Speculative decoding emits several tokens at once, which is why SLOs-Serve measures TPOT every 10 tokens. The identity E2E = TTFT + (n − 1)·TPOT holds per request; it does not hold between a P99 E2E and the P99s of its parts.

04 · Metric listThe metric list, derived from the papers

The papers use fourteen serving metrics plus mechanism proxies; four go beyond the usual list.

The project brief listed the metrics we expected to find: TTFT, TPS, TBT, TTLT, TPS/user and goodput. The list below was instead derived from every definition the extractors copied out of the 143 full-text records available when the taxonomy was built (133 optimizers and 10 metric papers) [P/Syn] DEFINITIONS.md §0. The consensus definitions are ours; the variants are what the papers wrote. Fourteen are serving metrics in the sense that a user or operator of a serving system would recognise them. Eight more families are measured beside them: cost, energy, memory, quality, fairness, utilization, speculative acceptance and mechanism proxies.

Four additions

Single-sequence decode speed deserves its own row because 33 papers (28 of them assigned to the variants in Table 1) report per-token time, tokens per second or a speedup for one sequence (or a fixed batch) with no arrival process [D] DEFINITIONS.md §1.6. It is an algorithm or kernel speed. Calling it "latency" invites comparison with serving latencies that contain queueing, which it does not.

Program or workflow completion time appears in 14 papers (13 assigned to variants), almost all 2024–26 agent, compound and RAG systems. Parrot states the reason: "the system should minimize the end-to-end time it takes to receive the final summary, rather than the latency of individual requests." [P] arXiv 2405.19888 §3

The token-timeline QoE family (7 papers, 2023–25) mostly scores how the token stream tracks a per-token deadline schedule: Andes's QoE, Etalon's fluidity index, smooth goodput and per-token deadline attainment [D] DEFINITIONS.md §1.14. Two members do not use a per-token schedule: S-LoRA's soft first-token satisfaction and the time to first visible token. These exist because mean TPOT hides mid-stream stalls (§12). Mechanism proxies are quantities such as cache hit rate or model start-up time that some papers optimize directly, because they are what the mechanism controls. They are not serving metrics, so the table lists them below the fourteen.

Undefined headline metrics

40 of the 143 full-text papers (28 %) use a headline or primary metric that they never define anywhere; 50 (35 %) do if loosely defined metrics, such as a TTFT without its queueing scope, are included [D] DEFINITIONS.md §3. Throughput (14 undefined) and TTFT (15) lead. Lodestar's TTFT "is never given a formal definition" in the extractor's words, yet it is the router's reward. An automated regex screen is looser and flags 54 of 143. Any count of "what papers optimize", including ours, therefore carries definitional uncertainty that the variant level in §13 makes visible.

05 · What is optimizedObjectives and constraints by metric family

Throughput is the commonest objective; token pace and quality are mostly constraints.

The picture has three parts. Throughput is the commonest objective at 57 of 137 (42 % [33.7–50.0]) and is almost never a constraint (2). Single-sequence speed, memory, goodput/attainment and hardware efficiency follow at 24–25 papers each (about 18 %). Token pace and quality flip sides: token pace is a constraint in 26 (19 % [13.3–26.4]) and an objective in 8; quality is a constraint in 36 (26 % [19.6–34.2]) and an objective in 3 [D] obj_con_by_family.json. Quality is usually phrased as "lossless" or as a bounded drop from the baseline.

Most papers have more than one objective family: 1 in 49 papers, 2 in 62, 3 in 19 and 4 in 7 [D] objectives_per_paper@1–4. Shares in Figure 3 therefore sum to well over 100 %.

Latency families split between objective and constraint

TTFT is an objective in 19 papers (14 %) and a constraint in 20 (15 %). Request latency (E2E, normalized, queueing) is an objective in 19 and a constraint in 10. Counting a "latency family" as first token, token pace, request latency or program latency, latency is an objective in 45 papers and a constraint in 38 [D] paper_table.csv; ORCH_NOTES.

The TTFT split follows layer: TTFT is an objective in 4 of 8 prefix-caching papers and 4 of 13 routers, and a constraint in 7 of 23 schedulers and 5 of 8 P/D systems [D] layer_family_heatmap.json. Of the 19 request-latency objective papers, 17 are evaluated under online arrivals, 1 offline and 1 mixed; six offline batch wall times were recoded out of this family during the re-pass [D] summary_v3.txt.

"Throughput under latency constraints" is a scheduler pattern

Only 20 of the 57 throughput-objective papers also state a latency constraint (TTFT, token pace or request latency) [D] paper_table.csv; ORCH_NOTES.

Widening the frame to "maximize throughput, goodput or cost subject to latency targets", 82 papers have one of those objectives and 42 of them have a latency constraint or a goodput objective, about half. Seven of the 42 constrain only E2E or request latency; counting only TTFT and token-pace constraints plus goodput objectives gives 35 [D] R5 recount. The form is commonest among schedulers and P/D systems, and even there it covers about half: 21 of 23 schedulers make throughput or goodput an objective, 12 of 23 state a latency constraint (13 counting goodput objectives), and so do 5 of 8 P/D systems [D] paper_table.csv.

Abstract track: a low-confidence robustness check

The 55 abstract-only records give the same ordering at the top: throughput is an objective in 24 (44 %), request latency in 19 (35 %), single-sequence speed in 10 (18 %), and quality is a constraint in 8 abstract only [D] summary_v2.txt l.38–50. Request latency looks larger there because abstracts say "latency" without saying which; the full-text coding splits that word into TTFT, token pace, request latency and single-sequence speed. Abstract records also record few constraints, since abstracts rarely state them.

What the slide simplifies

Shares depend on the family grouping. Merging goodput into throughput would widen throughput's lead; splitting throughput into tokens and requests would narrow it. The ordering of the top families did not change under the groupings we tried [Syn] SYNTHESIS v6.1 §1. Constraint shares are the noisiest part of the coding: in blind re-extraction, constraint sets matched exactly in only 14 of 30 papers, mostly over whether SLO attainment is listed beside TTFT and TPOT constraints [D] qa/QA_COMPARISON_v2.txt. The four smallest families (cache hit rate 5, fairness 2, start-up / scale time 2, user-perceived QoE 2 as objectives) are listed but carry no claim.

06 · The constraint sets the numberA throughput number needs its latency bound

Tightening the TPOT bound from 50 to 15 ms cuts CloudMatrix384's decode throughput 3.6-fold.

The paper states its goal as a throughput under a latency bound: "We assess absolute decode throughput (tokens/s) targeting a time-per-output-token (TPOT) SLO of below 50 ms" [P] arXiv 2506.12708 §5.2. At 50 ms it runs 96 sequences per NPU and reaches 1,943 tokens/s per NPU (achieved TPOT 49.4 ms). At 30 ms it drops to batch 24 and 974 tokens/s; at 15 ms to batch 8 and 538 tokens/s (achieved 14.9 ms) [P] Table 4. The ratio 1,943 / 538 = 3.61 [D]. The same system and model give throughputs a factor 3.6 apart depending only on the bound.

The mechanism, with the arithmetic

In decode, each running sequence emits one token per TPOT on average, so throughput per NPU ≈ batch × 1,000 / TPOT(ms). CloudMatrix-Infer decodes with multi-token prediction (MTP) with "an assumed effective acceptance rate of 70% for a single speculative token", so a decode step can emit more than one token per sequence; the identity holds because TPOT is measured per output token [P] arXiv 2506.12708 §5.2. Checking the printed rows [D]: 96 × 1,000 / 49.4 = 1,943; 24 × 1,000 / 24.6 = 976 (printed 974); 8 × 1,000 / 14.9 = 537 (printed 538). The identity holds within 0.3 %, which also confirms that the printed batch is per NPU.

A tighter bound forces a smaller batch, because each token must arrive sooner, and a smaller batch emits fewer tokens per second. Going from the 50 ms to the 15 ms point cuts the batch 12× (96 → 8) but the achieved TPOT only 3.3× (49.4 → 14.9 ms), so throughput falls by their ratio, 12 / 3.3 ≈ 3.6× [D].

The other axis of the same trade-off is the per-user token rate, TPS/user ≈ 1,000 / achieved TPOT: about 20, 41 and 67 tokens/s per user at the three bounds [D] 1,000/49.4, 1,000/24.6, 1,000/14.9. A system that reports only tokens/s per NPU hides this axis. Only 4 records carry the TPS/user code (DeepSpeed-FastGen's per-client rate SLA, Helix Parallelism, WaferLLM and Step-3's 20 tokens/s SLA), 6 define or use a per-stream token rate, and 2 more use "TPS" for system tokens/s [D] summary_v3.txt; DEFINITIONS.md §1.7. Helix states the frontier explicitly: it characterizes "the full Pareto frontier between throughput per gpu (tokens/sec/gpu) vs. interactivity (tokens/sec/user)" [P] arXiv 2507.07120 §3, via DEFINITIONS.md R5.

Workload also sets the number

At the same 50 ms bound, shorter sequences free KV-cache space and allow larger batches: 2,733 tokens/s per NPU at prompt 1,024 / output 1,024 (batch 128, achieved 46.8 ms) and 2,360 at 2,048 / 256 (batch 112, 47.4 ms) [P] Table 4. The batch-128 row was re-checked against the rendered table before printing; the paper's text "batch size of 96" refers to the 4,096/256 row. Both rows satisfy the batch × 1,000 / TPOT identity (2,735 and 2,363) [D].

07 · Technique layerWhat each layer optimizes

The technique layer largely sets the objective: schedulers chase throughput, speculative decoders single-sequence speed.

Each layer has a dominant objective [D] layer_family_heatmap.json. Request scheduling makes throughput an objective in 14 of 23 papers and goodput or attainment in 9. Speculative decoding makes single-sequence speed an objective in 9 of 13, with quality a constraint in 10. Agent and compound serving targets program latency in 10 of 16. Kernels target hardware efficiency in 6 of 8. Quantization and KV-compression papers target memory (4 of 7 and 5 of 7) and hold quality as a constraint (7 of 7 and 6 of 7). Energy/cost papers all make cost or energy the objective (4 of 4); two of the four also hold TTFT and token-pace constraints.

Routers are mixed

The 13 cluster-routing and placement papers split evenly. First token, request latency, throughput and cost each appear as an objective in 4, goodput or attainment in 3; only 3 state a TTFT constraint and 2 a token-pace constraint [D] cluster-routing/placement rows. Only two routers make TTFT the routing objective outright: Lodestar (reward −TTFT) and GORGO (fitness −p95 TTFT). Lodestar explains its choice. TPOT and end-to-end latency are observed "much further in the future than the routing decision time", and "they are affected by one more unknown factor, the number of decode tokens" [P] arXiv 2606.00946 §4.1. This argument matters for any router whose reward involves TPOT or E2E (§11, Table 7).

Serving-aware speculative decoding

Three of the 13 speculative-decoding papers move from single-sequence speed to serving objectives. AdaServe and AdaSpec target goodput or attainment under token-pace SLOs and are evaluated under online arrivals [D] paper_table.csv. AdaServe measures goodput as "the number of tokens generated per second for requests that successfully attain their SLO" [P] arXiv 2501.12162 §6.1. TurboSpec also uses the word "goodput", but its goodput counts accepted tokens per second with no SLO: "in the absence of speculative decoding, the goodput and throughput are the same" [P] arXiv 2406.14066 §1. The other 10 report single-sequence or offline-batch results only (§8).

08 · Load regimeUnder which load a paper is evaluated

60 of 137 papers never evaluate under online arrivals; their results exclude queueing.

Each record carries one load class, coded with a verbatim supporting quote during the re-pass [D] qa/REPASS_PROMPT.md item 3. Online arrivals means requests arrive over time (Poisson, gamma, trace replay or a rate sweep). Offline batch means a fixed batch, or all requests at t = 0. Single sequence means batch 1 or a fixed small batch with per-sequence latency or speed. Mixed means the main claims use more than one of these, including an online component. Across the 137: online arrivals 63, mixed 14, offline batch 32, single sequence 25, analytical or simulated only 3 [D] load_class.json · ALL|*. The never-online group is 32 + 25 + 3 = 60 (43.8 %).

Which layers never see arrivals

All 7 quantization papers and all 7 KV-compression papers are never evaluated online (14 of 14), as are 11 of 13 parallelism/offload/hardware papers, 10 of 13 speculative-decoding papers (single sequence 7, offline batch 3), 6 of 8 kernel papers and 3 of 4 MoE papers [D] load_class.json · <layer>|_never_online. At the other end, all 13 routers, all 4 energy/cost papers and all 3 LoRA papers are evaluated under online or mixed load, as are 19 of 23 schedulers.

Why offline gains do not transfer to serving

A speed-up measured without arrivals excludes queueing by construction. Under load, queueing becomes part of TTFT and E2E, and its size, like the batch a server actually runs, depends on the arrival rate. A method that shortens each decode step at batch 1 can help less, or not at all, when the server is already batching many requests, because the spare compute that the method exploits at batch 1 is used by the batch instead [Syn]. Section 9 shows this for EAGLE-3. The reverse also holds: a scheduler's latency gain under load does not say how fast a single sequence runs. So the speed claims of the two groups cannot be put on one axis.

Request latency and the recoded batch times

Of the 19 papers with a request-latency objective, 17 are evaluated under online arrivals, 1 offline and 1 mixed [D] summary_v3.txt. That clean split is partly by design: during the re-pass, six objectives labelled "end-to-end latency" that were really the wall time of an offline batch were recoded as OTHER:batch_time (SmoothQuant, FlexGen, H2O, InfiniGen, Helium, and dLoRA's migration objective) [D] summary_v3.txt. SmoothQuant's case shows why: "We measure the end-to-end latency of generating all hidden states for a batch of 4 sentences in one pass, i.e., the context stage latency." [P] arXiv 2211.10438 §5.3

The recode and its effect on denominators

During deck preparation the architect flagged that the "mixed" class contained papers without any arrival process. DeepSpeed-Inference, SGLang, EAGLE-3 and KVFlow were recoded from mixed to offline batch, and the R5 reviewer confirmed that none of the four texts shows an arrival process [D] ORCH_NOTES; notes/R5_review.md. The never-online count rose by 4 to 60, and the online-or-mixed population used in §10–§11 fell from 81 to 77. Every number in this report uses the post-recode values.

What the slide simplifies

"Never evaluate under online arrivals" does not mean "never test under load": an offline batch at the memory limit is a heavy load, and 32 papers use it. The claim is only that their reported speed-ups contain no queueing. Load class is one label per paper; a paper whose headline is single-sequence but which adds one small online experiment is "mixed". Load class is separate from whether the evaluation ran on hardware: the analytical/simulated-only class (3: RAGO, Helix Parallelism, NeuPIMs) has no arrival process, while the OLP router and PolyServe are simulation studies with simulated request arrivals and are coded online arrivals [D] paper_table.csv eval_type.

09 · Batch size changes the gainOne paper, two baselines, two regimes

In SGLang, EAGLE-3's throughput gain shrinks from 1.81× at batch 2 to 1.38× at 64.

The abstract reads: "EAGLE-3 achieves a speedup ratio up to 6.5x, with about 1.4x improvement over EAGLE-2. In the SGLang framework, EAGLE-3 achieves a 1.38x throughput improvement at a batch size of 64." [P] arXiv 2503.01840 abstract The two numbers in that sentence come from different experiments with different baselines. The 6.5× is the largest cell of Table 1 (6.47×, Vicuna-13B on HumanEval at temperature 0), a single-sequence speedup over vanilla autoregressive decoding. Table 1's cells run from 3.27× to 6.47× at temperature 0 and from 2.99× to 5.15× at temperature 1; the paper's per-model means at temperature 0 are 5.51×, 4.44×, 4.12× and 4.16× [P] Table 1; data rows spd@*.

The 1.38× comes from Table 3, where the SGLang team ran EAGLE and EAGLE-3 inside SGLang at fixed batch sizes against SGLang without speculation. EAGLE-3's gain is 1.81× at batch 2 and 1.82× at batch 4, then falls to 1.48× at 16 and 1.32–1.39× from batch 24 to 64. The first EAGLE falls below 1× from batch 24 (0.93×) and stays at or below 0.99× up to batch 64 [P] Table 3. At batch 1, Table 4 prints raw tokens/s (SGLang 158.34, with EAGLE-2 244.10, with EAGLE-3 373.25), which gives 2.36× for EAGLE-3 and 1.54× for EAGLE-2 [D] 373.25/158.34; 244.10/158.34; neither ratio is printed.

Mechanism

Speculative decoding trades extra computation (draft and verification) for fewer sequential steps. At batch 1, decode is limited by memory bandwidth and the accelerator has spare compute, so the extra work is nearly free and the step reduction becomes wall-clock speedup. As the batch grows, the batch itself uses that spare compute, verification of rejected draft tokens costs real throughput, and the gain shrinks [Syn]. The EAGLE-3 paper says the same: "As batch sizes increase, this redundancy decreases, reducing the effectiveness of speculative sampling." [P] arXiv 2503.01840 §4.3 Table 3 does not explain why EAGLE-3 stays above 1.3× where EAGLE falls below 1×, and we do not infer a cause. EAGLE's own paper says "EAGLE primarily focuses on latency rather than throughput" [P] arXiv 2401.15077 §4, Metrics.

Other speculative decoders: one agrees, one reverses

SpecInfer reports the same direction: "SpecInfer's performance improvement over existing systems reduces as the batch size (i.e., number of concurrent requests) increases", because it uses spare GPU resources that a larger batch consumes [P] arXiv 2305.09781 §6.2.

MagicDec reports the reverse beyond a "critical sequence length": there speculative decoding "can achieve higher speedups for larger batches", up to 2.51× for LLaMA-3.1-8B at batch sizes 32 to 256, which the paper explains by the KV-cache loading bottleneck outweighing the added verification compute at long contexts [P] arXiv 2408.11049 §1, abstract. So batch size changes the gain, in a direction that depends on context length. At a fixed batch, a per-token speedup is also a throughput speedup; that identity no longer holds once requests arrive over time [Syn] DEFINITIONS.md R6. EAGLE-3's Table 5 (vLLM, batch 2–56) was not extracted: its caption says A100 while the §4.4 text says RTX 3090 [D] data caveats.

What the slide simplifies

Table 3 is a fixed-batch throughput experiment with no request arrivals, which is why EAGLE-3 is coded offline-batch (§8). So even the 1.38× is not a serving gain under load: it says nothing about TTFT, queueing or tails. "6.5× falls to 1.38×" would be wrong twice: the baselines differ (vanilla autoregressive vs SGLang) and the hardware for Table 1 is not stated on its page. Table 3 covers one model, one dataset and one GPU.

10 · Change over timeHas the framing moved toward SLOs?

Within online/mixed-load papers, SLO framing held flat; only capacity-at-a-latency-limit definitions significantly declined.

The hypothesis we started with (H1) was that serving papers moved from throughput and normalized latency in 2022–23 to SLO-bound metrics from 2024. Across all 137 papers the SLO-framed share does rise: 6 of 34 (18 %) in 2022–23, 16 of 57 (28 %) in 2024, 21 of 46 (46 %) in 2025–26, a significant trend (z = 2.72, p = 0.006) [D] trend_online.json · all|slo_framed|*. Within the 77 papers evaluated under online or mixed load, it is flat: 4 of 8 (50 %), 15 of 35 (43 %), 16 of 34 (47 %), p = 0.95 [D] online_mixed|slo_framed|*.

About half of the corpus-wide rise is composition

Recent papers in our sample are more often serving systems evaluated under load. Hold the pre-2025 SLO-framed rates within each load group fixed (online or mixed 19 of 43; never online 3 of 48) and apply them to the 2025–26 mix (34 online or mixed, 12 not): the prediction is (34 × 19/43 + 12 × 3/48) / 46 = 34 % [D] ORCH_NOTES "Composition".

Against the pooled 2022–24 rate of 24 % (22 of 91), the observed 46 % is a rise of 22 points, of which composition accounts for 10, about half. Measured from 2022–23 alone (8 online papers), the same calculation predicts 39 % and composition is about three-quarters of the rise [D] R5 recount. The remainder comes mostly from never-online 2025–26 papers that design to latency bounds (RAGO, MegaScale-Infer, CloudMatrix384, Step-3, DREX): 5 of 12, against 3 of 48 before 2025.

How tiers are assigned

The function tier() in scripts/analysis_v3.py reads each record's serving-latency entries (TTFT, token pace, E2E, normalized latency, queueing, program latency) with their role and statistic. Papers evaluated only on single sequences go to tier 5, because batch-1 latency is not serving latency. Tier 1 comes from two hand-set lists (strict and lenient) built from the tail audit and reviews R2–R3. "Distributional" is not always "tail"

Many attainment targets count requests whose mean TPOT meets a threshold. They constrain how per-request means spread across requests, and say nothing about token-level stalls within a request. About 6–8 papers have such mean-TPOT SLOs: DistServe, semi-PD, TaiChi, TokenScale, SOLA and Aladdin, plus two mixed cases, AdaSpec and AdaServe, which bound each step in their design but score the SLO on each request's average per-token latency in evaluation.

About 13 constrain token-level timing directly: by a gap percentile in Splitwise, Sarathi-Serve, Mooncake (P90), DynamoLLM, ConServe, HyGen, Apt-Serve and GreenLLM (P95); by a per-iteration budget in Medha; in SLOs-Serve by a bound enforced per token and measured over 10-token windows; and by per-token deadlines in Aegaeon, JITServe and PolyServe, of which PolyServe is tier 1b [Syn/D] ORCH_NOTES, Erratum v6.1b; SYNTHESIS v6.1 §6. PolyServe's SLO is the deadline form "the i-th token must be produced before TTFT + i · TPOT"; it was counted as a mean-TPOT SLO before the v6.1 erratum. MLPerf's P99 TPOT gate is of the first kind: a P99 over requests of each request's mean TPOT.

Tier 3 is mostly "unspecified"

Only about 4–5 of the 32 tier-3 papers name the mean in a design quote (a script word match finds 5); corpus-wide, about 9 papers across all tiers do [D] tail_tiers.json · all|3_mean_named; R3 manual check. Of the 62 serving-latency objective entries, 30 leave the statistic unspecified and 26 state a mean somewhere, usually in the evaluation [D] summary_v3.txt. Tier 3 therefore merges "the mean" with "not said".

Tails in headlines are mostly reported results

The tail audit read 40 papers with recorded or likely tail entries. 26 report tails without targeting them, 9 use a tail percentile as an SLO, 4 had a tail-oriented design aim and 1 had no latency tail; those labels predate the tier refinements [D] tail_tiers.json · audit|*. Examples of reported-only tails: Llumnix (P99 TTFT and TPOT reported; its stated goal is "to improve prefill and decode latencies"), SpotServe (a "tail latency by 2.4 - 9.1×" headline), TraCT (a P99 that is P99 TTFT, since outputs are 3 tokens long) and Medha, whose 174× tail-latency headline is P99 in the abstract and P90 TTFT against LoongServe in Fig. 1a [P] qa/TAIL_AUDIT.md; notes/R5_review.md.

A tail objective can be gamed

GORGO tunes two routing weights with an evolution strategy: "GORGO proxy uses a simple (1+1) evolutionary strategy to tune weights Wrtt and Wqueue on the objective function, p95 TTFT." [P] arXiv 2602.11688 §2.3 In its main design the queue weight is restricted to [0.05, 0.5], and held-out p95 TTFT improves by 6.9–15.5 % and p95 E2E latency by 14.3–30.9 % over baseline policies [P] arXiv 2602.11688 abstract; evaluation, txt l.262–275.

Its Table 8 is a separate run, labelled reward hacking, that shows what happens when the queue weight has no lower bound and the evolution strategy drives it to zero (W_queue = 0): "the tuned gorgo-static wins every TTFT percentile but is worst on the E2E and ITL tails, because it routes ∼100% of traffic to a single replica that saturates under load." The caption adds that "ITL is the median" [P] Table 8.

With the bound removed, a single-metric tail objective was met by concentrating load. The authors note that the lower bound on the queue term "mitigates but does not eliminate this trade-off" [P] Limitations, txt l.604. For any router with a tail reward, this is the central risk.

12 · StallsDoes the statistic matter?

Etalon's capacity ranking flips with the metric: Sarathi-Serve leads on TPOT, trails on P99 TBT.

Etalon compares vLLM with Sarathi-Serve (vLLM with chunked prefill) under three capacity criteria at the same 25 ms target [P] arXiv 2407.07000 §5.2. By a TPOT criterion, Sarathi-Serve sustains 1.16 QPS and vLLM 0.66, a ratio of 1.76 [D] 1.16 / 0.66. By a P99-TBT criterion the order reverses: vLLM 0.65, Sarathi-Serve 0.34, a ratio of 1.9 [D] 0.65 / 0.34, which the paper describes as "Sarathi has a 2x lower token throughput compared to vLLM" by the tail-TBT metric. By Etalon's own fluidity index the two are equal: "fluidity-index based capacity is the same at 0.6 QPS" [P] §5.2. All bar values are chart reads ±0.02.

Why the statistic flips the order

TPOT is a per-request mean of gaps. Etalon: "Both these metrics normalise the latency by the number of decode tokens in the request. This normalization can mask the jitters that occur as intermittent stalls" [P] §3.1. A system that occasionally stalls a stream for a long time (for example, while a long prefill runs) can have a low mean TPOT and a high P99 TBT. Which system "wins" then depends on whether the criterion averages the stall away or counts it. Here the two criteria rank the systems in opposite orders, and a third criterion that tracks per-token deadlines rates them equal.

Etalon does not recommend P99 TBT as the fix. About the Fig. 3 setup it writes: "Solely examining tail latency disproportionately penalizes vLLM, although both systems have comparable median TBTs. Thus, while TPOT downplays the discrepancies between the systems, tail-TBT overstates them." [P] §3.1 Its answer is a per-token-deadline measure, the fluidity index. For reporting, this argues for P99 TBT beside P99 TPOT, and a deadline-based measure beside both.

The fluidity index

Etalon assigns each token a deadline Dᵢ = D_p + i·D_d, where D_p is a prefill target (a function of prompt length) and D_d a per-token decode target; the paper uses 25, 50 and 100 ms for D_d [P] §4.1; data rows c_deadline_def, c_Dd_targets. The fluidity index of a request is the fraction of its deadlines met. After a miss, later deadlines are reset, because otherwise "a single stall can amount to 10s of deadline-misses" [P] §4.1. The capacity criterion is that 99 % of requests have a fluidity index of at least 0.9 [P] row c_threshold. The scheduling slack added to D_p is not printed in the paper.

Figure 11 shows the same effect at a different operating point (high load). On TPOT-based throughput the systems are "at par" (about 17 vs 20 tokens/s, ratio 1.18); on tail-TBT-based throughput the paper says "the tail TBT-based throughput for vLLM is 3× worse than Sarathi due to huge generation stalls" [P] §5.2; DEFINITIONS.md §1.2–1.3.

The text gives vLLM's tail TBT as "1 second"; the inverse of our 1.4 tokens/s chart read is about 0.7 s, so the bar read and the text disagree somewhat. Fig. 3a shows a vLLM stall of about 10 s that hardly changes TPOT or normalized latency (extractor reading of Fig. 3a) [P] rows a_text_tbt_1s, a_text_stall_10s. The direction differs from Figure 10 (here Sarathi-Serve wins on the tail metric), which is the point: the two figures are different operating points and must not be mixed.

Two more metric papers

Andes describes a case where the user sees a pause mid-stream although "TTFT or average TPOT does not degrade; both the first and last tokens were delivered on time, hiding the long pause in the middle" [P] arXiv 2404.16283 §3.1. The smooth-goodput paper shows that existing metrics can be gamed: "manually delaying the delivery of some tokens can improve SLOs", and "actively abandoning requests that do not meet SLOs" can improve request-level metrics [P] arXiv 2410.14257 abstract. Its smooth goodput penalizes the maximum idle latency over a request's tokens instead of the average.

What the slide simplifies

This is one model (Llama-3-8B), one H100 and one dataset, with capacities read from a chart. It shows that a metric can reverse a ranking; it does not show how often rankings reverse across systems. Etalon shares several authors with Sarathi-Serve, and fluidity is its own proposed metric. The 25 ms target is the strictest of its three decode targets. None of this says TPOT is wrong; it says TPOT and P99 TBT measure different things and a design claim should name which one it optimizes.

13 · Same name, different quantityRival definitions under one name

One metric name, several quantities: TTFT, goodput and throughput each have rival definitions.

Three headline metrics carry most of the ambiguity [D] def_variants.json. TTFT starts at arrival in 16 papers and is defined as prefill duration in 15 (5 of which measure it under load, so queueing is in fact included); 7 measure it offline or for a single request; 20 state no start point. That 20 is the script's list over the 143 records after the re-pass; the DEFINITIONS hand list had 19, and the two lists differ in membership, which is why prose elsewhere says 19–20. Two papers (CacheGen and the reasoning-serving study RLLM) fall in both T1 and T2, because they define TTFT one way and measure it another.

Goodput has at least 8 definitions; the modal one, "max request rate at X % attainment", is used by 13, with X = 90 in 11. Token throughput counts input plus output tokens in 7, output tokens over prefill plus decode time in 13, decode only in 8, prefill only in 8, and leaves the accounting unstated in 8.

A.1 Pipeline and agents

Discovery. Selection. Tracks in SELECTION.tsv: full 137 (133 optimizers selected first, plus GORGO, the OLP router, PPD and ExeGPT added after R1 by agent E16), mp-full 10 (metric, benchmark and critique papers read for definitions), abstract 60 (55 usable records; 5 marked unclear), excluded 7 (simulators, characterizations and a latency model, which are not optimizers). Appendix DGlossary

Attainment (SLO attainment)
The fraction of requests (or tokens) that meet their latency SLOs, e.g. 90 % of requests with TTFT and TPOT under their bounds.
Capacity
The highest offered request rate a system sustains under a stated condition. "Capacity at a latency limit" uses a knee of a latency curve or a latency target with no attainment fraction (DEFINITIONS variants Q4, Q5).
Cochran–Armitage test
A test for a linear trend in a proportion across ordered groups (here three periods); see A.6.
Constraint
What a method must respect: an SLO, a quality floor, a memory budget.
Decode / prefill
Prefill processes the prompt and produces the first token; decode generates the remaining tokens one step at a time.
E2E (TTLT, request JCT)
Arrival to last output token, per request.
Family
A group of canonical metric codes used for counting (16: 15 named plus mechanism/other).
Fluidity index
Etalon's per-request fraction of per-token deadlines Dᵢ = D_p + i·D_d met, with deadlines reset after a miss.
Goodput
Throughput counting only SLO-satisfying work. Modal form: the maximum request rate at which at least X % (usually 90 %) of requests meet all SLOs, optionally per GPU. Other forms in §13.
ITL
Inter-token latency; in most harnesses the same as TBT (pooled gaps between streamed chunks, where one chunk can carry several tokens), in NVIDIA's AIPerf the same as TPOT.
KV cache
The stored attention keys and values of previous tokens; its size limits the batch and context a server can hold, and a prefix cache reuses it across requests.
LoRA
Low-rank adaptation: small adapter weights on a shared base model; LoRA serving systems serve many adapters at once.
MoE
Mixture of experts: a model that routes each token to a few expert sub-networks.
Load class
The regime a paper is evaluated under: online arrivals, mixed, offline batch, single sequence, analytical/simulated only (§8).
NPU
Neural processing unit; here Huawei's Ascend accelerators in CloudMatrix384 (the paper does not say whether "NPU" means a package or a die).
Normalized latency
E2E divided by output tokens, averaged over requests; mixes TTFT and TPOT.
OLP router
The online-linear-programming router of Chen et al. (arXiv 2607.03948): bid-price routing to decode workers with per-request tail-threshold rewards, evaluated in Vidur.
Objective
What a method is designed to improve, backed by a verbatim design statement.
P/D disaggregation
Running prefill and decode on separate instances.
P99 (P90, P95)
The 99th (90th, 95th) percentile of a latency distribution; which distribution (over requests or over tokens) must be stated.
QoE
Quality of experience; here token-timeline measures such as Andes's QoE, which score the delivered token stream against an ideal reading-pace timeline.
QPS
Queries (requests) per second, the offered or sustained request rate.
Purposive sample
Papers chosen by criteria (uptake, venue, recency), not at random; shares describe the sample only.
Reported metric
Evaluated or claimed in a headline but not targeted by the design.
Single-sequence speed
Per-token time, tokens/s or speedup of one sequence (or a fixed small batch) without request arrivals.
SLO
Service-level objective: a latency bound, possibly with a percentile or attainment level.
SLO-framed
Our label for a paper with a goodput/attainment objective or a TTFT, token-pace or request-latency constraint.
TBT
Time between tokens: each inter-token gap is one sample; its P99 is a tail over tokens.
Tier (tail tier)
Our classification of how a design treats the latency statistic: 1a percentile minimized, 1b tail named as aim, 2a distributional target, 2b deterministic bound, 3 mean or unspecified, 5 no serving-latency target (§11).
TPOT
Time per output token: a request's mean decode gap, first token excluded; its P99 is a tail over requests.
TPS/user
Tokens per second delivered to one stream, ≈ 1/TPOT; the interactivity axis.
TTFT
Time to first token: arrival to first output token, including queueing (modal definition); some papers use prefill duration instead.
Vidur
An LLM-serving simulator (arXiv 2405.05465), used by the OLP router in this corpus.
Wilson interval
A confidence interval for a proportion that behaves well for small counts; see A.6.
